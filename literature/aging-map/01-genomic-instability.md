# 01 — Genomic instability

> Primary hallmark ("damage") according to López-Otín et al., *Cell* 2023 ([doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001)).

## 1. Definition

Accumulation, over a lifetime, of lesions and alterations of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA (breaks, point mutations, rearrangements, aneuploidies, retrotransposon insertions) that exceed the repair capacities of the cell. It meets the three criteria of a hallmark: it increases with age, its experimental aggravation accelerates aging, and its reduction slows it down, at least in animals (López-Otín 2013, [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2013.05.039](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2013.05.039); Schumacher 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03307-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03307-7)).

*In plain terms: DNA is the "instruction manual" of every cell. It is damaged tens of thousands of times a day; the cell repairs almost everything, but not everything. Uncorrected errors accumulate, like typos that add up each time a manual is copied.*

## 2. Mechanisms and sources

**Sources of damage.**
- *Endogenous*: replication errors, spontaneous hydrolysis (depurination, deamination), reactive oxygen species from mitochondrial metabolism, aldehydes, replication/transcription conflicts.
- *Exogenous*: UV, ionising radiation, tobacco, chemical agents and chemotherapies.

**Nature of the accumulated alterations.**
- **Somatic point mutations**: their number grows linearly with age in virtually all normal tissues. Sun-exposed skin already carries thousands of mutations per cell and clones under positive selection for cancer genes (Martincorena 2015, [doi:10.1126/science.aaa6806](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa6806)). Single-molecule sequencing (NanoSeq) confirms a steady accumulation, including in cells that do not divide (Abascal 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03477-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03477-4)). Neurons also accumulate mutations, faster in neurodegeneration (Lodato 2018, [doi:10.1126/science.aao4426](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao4426)).
- **Correlation with species longevity**: across 16 mammals, the annual somatic mutation rate is inversely proportional to lifespan, so that at the end of life all animals carry a comparable number of mutations (Cagan 2022, [doi:10.1038/s41586-022-04618-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04618-z)). A strong argument for a role of DNA in the pace of aging, but not proof of causality.
- **Double-strand breaks** and the efficiency of their repair: the sirtuin SIRT6 is more active in long-lived species and improves repair (Tian 2019, [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2019.03.043](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2019.03.043)); its overexpression extends the life of male mice (Kanfi 2012, [doi:10.1038/nature10815](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10815)).
- **Aneuploidy and chromosomal mosaicism**: loss of the Y chromosome in blood (LOY) affects a large share of older men and is associated with increased mortality (Forsberg 2014, [doi:10.1038/ng.2966](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.2966)); in mice, it causes cardiac fibrosis and heart failure, which suggests causality (Sano 2022, [doi:10.1126/science.abn3100](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abn3100)). Mice deficient in BubR1 (spindle checkpoint) age prematurely; its overexpression extends healthspan and lifespan (Baker 2004, [doi:10.1038/ng1382](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng1382); Baker 2012, [doi:10.1038/ncb2643](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncb2643)).
- **Retrotransposons**: LINE-1 elements, normally silenced, reactivate in senescent cells and in aged tissues; their cytoplasmic DNA triggers an inflammatory interferon response (De Cecco 2019, [doi:10.1038/s41586-018-0784-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0784-9); Simon 2019, [doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2019.02.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.02.014)).
- **Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)**: more exposed, less protected. "Mutator" mice (POLG polymerase without proofreading function) accumulate mtDNA mutations and age prematurely (Trifunovic 2004, [doi:10.1038/nature02517](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02517); Kujoth 2005, [doi:10.1126/science.1112125](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1112125)). The role of mtDNA mutations in normal aging remains debated (Kauppila 2017, [doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.09.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2016.09.017)).
- **Nuclear envelope**: lamin A maintains the architecture of the nucleus; its mutated form (progerin) deforms the nucleus and disrupts repair and chromatin organisation (Eriksson 2003, [doi:10.1038/nature01629](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01629)).

**Cellular consequences.** Unrepaired damage leads to (i) a transmitted mutation, (ii) cell-cycle arrest and senescence, (iii) cell death, or (iv) clonal selection. The persistent DNA damage response (DDR) is a major driver of senescence and inflammation (Schumacher 2021).

## 3. Symptoms and clinical manifestations

**Human progeroid syndromes (monogenic accelerated aging)** — the most direct evidence in humans:

- **Werner syndrome** (*WRN* gene, repair helicase): grey hair, cataracts, diabetes, atherosclerosis, cancers from age 20-30 (Yu 1996, [doi:10.1126/science.272.5259.258](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.272.5259.258)).
- **Hutchinson-Gilford progeria** (*LMNA*, progerin): accelerated aging in children, death from cardiovascular disease around age 14-15 (Eriksson 2003).
- **ERCC1-XPF deficiency** (nucleotide excision repair): severe progeria; in mice, these lesions redirect metabolism towards a "survival" mode close to that of caloric restriction (Niedernhofer 2006, [doi:10.1038/nature05456](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05456)). Deleting *Ercc1* in immune cells alone is enough to make solid organs age (Yousefzadeh 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03547-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03547-7)).

**Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP)**: blood clones carrying mutations (*DNMT3A*, *TET2*, *ASXL1*…) expand in >10 % of people over 70. They increase the risk of blood cancer, overall mortality (Jaiswal 2014, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1408617](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1408617)) and cardiovascular risk (Jaiswal 2017, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1701719](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1701719)); in mice, *Tet2*-deficient macrophages accelerate atherosclerosis through inflammation (IL-1β/NLRP3) (Fuster 2017, [doi:10.1126/science.aag1381](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aag1381)).

**GWAS**: age at menopause, a marker of ovarian aging, is strongly associated with variants in DNA repair genes; manipulating them in mice changes the ovarian reserve (Ruth 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03779-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03779-7)).

**Associated age-related diseases**: cancers (the main one), cardiovascular diseases (CHIP, LOY), neurodegeneration (neuronal mutations), ovarian failure, immunosenescence.

## 4. Interactions with the other hallmarks

| Hallmark (2023 numbering) | Link with genomic instability |
|---|---|
| 2. Telomere attrition | Telomeres are a specialised form of DNA damage that is hard to repair; persistent DDR at telomeres (see file 02). |
| 3. Epigenetic alterations | Break repair redistributes chromatin proteins and erodes epigenetic information (Yang 2023, [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.12.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.12.027)); loss of heterochromatin → awakening of LINE-1. |
| 4. Loss of proteostasis | Mutations produce misfolded proteins; some repair proteins depend on chaperones. |
| 6. Deregulated nutrient sensing | Damage represses the GH/IGF-1 axis (survival response) (Niedernhofer 2006); repair consumes NAD+ (PARP) at the expense of sirtuins (Covarrubias 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41580-020-00313-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-020-00313-x)). |
| 7. Mitochondrial dysfunction | mtDNA mutations → failing respiratory chain → more free radicals → more damage (debated vicious circle). |
| 8. Cellular senescence | Persistent DDR is the main trigger of senescence. |
| 9. Stem cell exhaustion | Damage exhausts stem cells or favours their clonal drift (CHIP). |
| 10. Altered intercellular communication | The secretome of damaged/senescent cells alters neighbouring tissues. |
| 11. Chronic inflammation | Cytoplasmic DNA (LINE-1, micronuclei) → cGAS-STING pathway → interferons (De Cecco 2019); CHIP → IL-1β. |
| 12. Dysbiosis | Some bacteria produce genotoxins: colibactin from *pks+ E. coli* leaves a distinct mutational signature in organoids and in human colorectal cancers (Pleguezuelos-Manzano 2020, [doi:10.1038/s41586-020-2080-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2080-8)). The link with normal aging remains indirect. |

## 5. Existing or investigational remedies

| Intervention | Target | Level of evidence | Key result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lonafarnib | Inhibits farnesylation of progerin (HGPS) | **Approved** (FDA 2020, progeria) | Reduced mortality in treated vs untreated children (cohort study) | Gordon 2018, [doi:10.1001/jama.2018.3264](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.3264) |
| Base editing (ABE) of the *LMNA* mutation | DNA correction | Preclinical (mice) | A single injection corrects the mutation, reduces progerin and doubles median survival | Koblan 2021, [doi:10.1038/s41586-020-03086-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-03086-7) |
| NAD+ precursors (NMN, NR) | Restore NAD+ (PARP, sirtuins, repair) | Solid preclinical; human phase 1-2 trials | Mice: lessening of age-related declines (Mills 2016); humans: NR safe and raises NAD+ (Martens 2018); NMN improves muscle insulin sensitivity in prediabetic women (Yoshino 2021). No demonstrated benefit on DNA repair in humans. | [doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2016.09.013); [doi:10.1038/s41467-018-03421-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-03421-7); [doi:10.1126/science.abe9985](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abe9985) |
| SIRT6 activation | Double-strand break repair | Preclinical (transgenic mice) | Overexpression: increased longevity in male mice | Kanfi 2012 |
| BubR1 overexpression | Chromosome segregation | Preclinical (mice) | Less aneuploidy and fewer cancers, longer life | Baker 2012 |
| Reverse transcriptase inhibitors (e.g. lamivudine) | Block LINE-1 | Preclinical | Reduced interferon-type inflammation in old mice | De Cecco 2019 |
| Prevention of exposure (UV, tobacco) | Reduce exogenous damage | Established (epidemiology) | Fewer skin and lung cancers | consensus, not specific to aging |
| Targeting CHIP clones / IL-1β | CHIP-related inflammation | Hypothesis; secondary analyses of trials | Exploratory analysis of the CANTOS trial: in carriers of *TET2* CHIP, canakinumab (anti-IL-1β) reduces cardiovascular events (HR 0.38; 95 % CI 0.15-0.96), non-significant interaction | Svensson 2022, [doi:10.1001/jamacardio.2022.0386](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2022.0386) |

**Finding**: no intervention has been shown in humans to slow the accumulation of somatic mutations. The only clinical successes concern monogenic diseases (progeria).

## 6. Open questions

1. **Causality in normal aging**: are somatic mutations numerous enough to explain functional decline, or mainly a cancer risk? The mutations/species-longevity parallel (Cagan 2022) does not prove causality.
2. **Damage vs mutation**: is it the persistent lesion (DDR signal, senescence) or the fixed mutation that matters most? Schumacher 2021 leans towards lesions.
3. **mtDNA**: do mutations reach a functional threshold in normal human aging?
4. **Clonal mosaicism** (CHIP, LOY, clones in the oesophagus or skin): causal factor of disease or mere marker? Should it be treated?
5. **Improving repair** without favouring the survival of cancer cells: no validated strategy in humans.
6. **Biomarkers**: how can the damage burden be measured routinely (single-molecule sequencing, LOY, CHIP)?
