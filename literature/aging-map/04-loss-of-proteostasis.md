# 04 — Loss of proteostasis

> Primary hallmark ("damage") according to López-Otín et al., *Cell* 2023 ([doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001)).

## 1. Definition

Age-related decline of the network that ensures the cell's proteins are made, folded into the right shape, repaired and destroyed when damaged. Result: accumulation of misfolded proteins and toxic aggregates. Aggregation diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS, amyloidoses) are its most visible manifestation (Hipp 2019, [doi:10.1038/s41580-019-0101-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-019-0101-y); Labbadia & Morimoto 2015, [doi:10.1146/annurev-biochem-060614-033955](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-biochem-060614-033955)).

*In plain terms: proteins are the cell's "machines". To work, they must fold like a precise origami. "Assistants" (chaperones) help with folding, "shredders" (proteasome, lysosome) recycle the failed pieces. With age, assistants and shredders run out of steam: the failed origamis pile up and form clumps that clog the cell.*

## 2. Mechanisms and sources

The proteostasis network comprises about 2,000 proteins organised in three branches (Hipp 2019):

- **Synthesis and folding**: ribosomes and **chaperones** (heat shock proteins, HSP70, HSP90, chaperonins). Their induction under stress depends on the factor HSF-1, whose response weakens with age.
- **Specialised stress responses**: the unfolded protein response of the endoplasmic reticulum (UPR-ER) and of mitochondria (UPR-mt), and the integrated stress response (ISR), which slows protein production when the cell is overwhelmed. With age, these responses become less effective or remain stuck in the "brake" position.
- **Degradation**: the **proteasome** (ubiquitin-proteasome system, which destroys proteins one by one) and the **lysosome** through autophagy. Macroautophagy is now a separate hallmark (see file 05); **chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA)**, which brings proteins one at a time into the lysosome through the LAMP2A receptor, declines with age (Kaushik & Cuervo 2018, [doi:10.1038/s41580-018-0001-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41580-018-0001-6)).

**What happens with age**
- **Widespread aggregation**: in the worm *C. elegans*, hundreds of proteins become insoluble with age, even in the absence of disease (David 2010, [doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1000450](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000450)); the proteome becomes imbalanced (Walther 2015, [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2015.03.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2015.03.032)).
- **Sources of damage**: oxidation, glycation, carbonylation, translation errors, somatic mutations (file 01), lower energy (ATP needed by chaperones and the proteasome).
- **Prion-like spread**: some aggregates (amyloid-β, tau, α-synuclein) act as "seeds" that convert healthy proteins and spread from cell to cell, which explains the progression of neurodegenerative diseases through the brain (Jucker & Walker 2013, [doi:10.1038/nature12481](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12481)).
- **Causality in animals**: boosting HSF-1 extends the worm's life; reducing it shortens it (Hsu 2003, [doi:10.1126/science.1083701](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1083701)). Restoring CMA (LAMP2A) in the liver of old mice reduces the accumulation of damaged proteins and improves liver function (Zhang & Cuervo 2008, [doi:10.1038/nm.1851](https://doi.org/10.1038/nm.1851)).

## 3. Symptoms and clinical manifestations

Human evidence comes mainly from **misfolding diseases** ("proteinopathies", more than 50 diseases; Chiti & Dobson 2017, [doi:10.1146/annurev-biochem-061516-045115](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-biochem-061516-045115)), whose risk soars with age:

| Disease | Aggregating protein | Link with age |
|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer's disease | Amyloid-β, tau | Risk roughly doubling every 5 years after 65 |
| Parkinson's disease | α-synuclein | Age = main risk factor |
| ALS (Charcot disease) | TDP-43, SOD1, FUS | Peak after 60 |
| Huntington's disease | Huntingtin (polyglutamine expansion) | Genetic; onset in adulthood |
| Transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) | Transthyretin (TTR) | "Wild-type" form common beyond 75, a cause of heart failure |
| Cataract | Lens crystallins | Almost universal with age |

**Genetic forms**: mutations that make a protein more unstable (e.g. *APP*, *PSEN1* for Alzheimer's; *TTR* for hereditary amyloidosis) bring forward the age of disease, which shows that aggregation is causal for these diseases (Chiti & Dobson 2017).

**Lysosomal diseases**: Niemann-Pick disease type C (accumulation of lipids in lysosomes) illustrates the link between chaperones and lysosomes (Kirkegaard 2010, [doi:10.1038/nature08710](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08710)).

## 4. Interactions with the other hallmarks

| Hallmark (2023 numbering) | Link with loss of proteostasis |
|---|---|
| 1. Genomic instability | Mutations and errors → defective proteins; repair proteins depend on chaperones. |
| 2. Telomere attrition | Mostly indirect link, through senescence. |
| 3. Epigenetic alterations | Chromatin regulates the expression of chaperones and HSF-1. |
| 5. Disabled macroautophagy | Main route for clearing large aggregates; the two hallmarks reinforce each other (file 05). |
| 6. Deregulated nutrient sensing | mTOR drives protein production; inhibiting it reduces the load and activates autophagy. The insulin/IGF-1 pathway acts through DAF-16 and HSF-1 in the worm (Hsu 2003). |
| 7. Mitochondrial dysfunction | Less ATP for chaperones and the proteasome; misfolded mitochondrial proteins → UPR-mt; aggregates that damage mitochondria. |
| 8. Cellular senescence | Aggregates and reticulum stress can trigger senescence. |
| 9. Stem cell exhaustion | Stem cells maintain enhanced proteostasis; its loss reduces their regenerative capacity. |
| 10. Altered intercellular communication | Spread of aggregates from cell to cell (Jucker & Walker 2013); stress signals transmitted between tissues in the worm. |
| 11. Chronic inflammation | Aggregates activate microglia and the NLRP3 inflammasome. |
| 12. Dysbiosis | In animals: the bacterial amyloid curli accelerates α-synuclein aggregation (old rats; Chen 2016, [doi:10.1038/srep34477](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep34477)); the microbiota worsens motor deficits in a mouse model of Parkinson's (Sampson 2016, [doi:10.1016/j.cell.2016.11.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2016.11.018)); pathological α-synuclein injected into the gut reaches the brain through the vagus nerve (Kim 2019, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2019.05.035](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.05.035)). The hypothesis that Parkinson's starts in the gut is not demonstrated in humans. |

## 5. Existing or investigational remedies

| Intervention | Target | Level of evidence | Key result | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tafamidis | Stabilises TTR (prevents its dissociation) | **Approved** (cardiac ATTR) | Phase 3 trial: lower all-cause mortality and fewer cardiovascular hospitalisations | Maurer 2018, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1805689](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1805689) |
| Patisiran (interfering RNA) | Reduces TTR production | **Approved** (hereditary ATTR, neuropathy) | Phase 3: improvement of neuropathy and quality of life vs placebo | Adams 2018, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1716153](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1716153) |
| NTLA-2001 (in vivo CRISPR-Cas9 editing) | Inactivates the *TTR* gene in the liver | Human phase 1 trial | A single infusion: mean TTR reduction of ~87 % at the 0.3 mg/kg dose | Gillmore 2021, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2107454](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2107454) |
| Lecanemab (antibody) | Clears amyloid-β protofibrils | **Approved** (early Alzheimer's) | Phase 3: clinical decline slowed by 27 % over 18 months; side effects (brain oedema and microbleeds, ARIA) | van Dyck 2023, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2212948](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2212948) |
| Donanemab (antibody) | Amyloid plaques | **Approved** (early Alzheimer's) | Phase 3: decline slowed by about a third in patients with low/intermediate tau load; ARIA | Sims 2023, [doi:10.1001/jama.2023.13239](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.13239) |
| Arimoclomol | Amplifies the chaperone response (HSP70) | **Approved** by the FDA on 20 September 2024 (Miplyffa, with miglustat, Niemann-Pick C) ([FDA press release](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-treatment-niemann-pick-disease-type-c)) | Phase 2/3: slower progression vs placebo | Mengel 2021, [doi:10.1002/jimd.12428](https://doi.org/10.1002/jimd.12428); mechanism: Kirkegaard 2010 |
| Sodium phenylbutyrate + taurursodiol (AMX0035) | Reticulum and mitochondrial stress | Positive phase 2; negative phase 3 (PHOENIX), withdrawn from the market in 2024 | Phase 2: slower functional decline in ALS; not confirmed afterwards | Paganoni 2020, [doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1916945](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1916945); withdrawal announced on 4 April 2024 after the failure of PHOENIX ([Amylyx press release](https://www.amylyx.com/news/amylyx-pharmaceuticals-announces-formal-intention-to-remove-relyvrior/albriozatm-from-the-market-provides-updates-on-access-to-therapy-pipeline-corporate-restructuring-and-strategy); [Federal Register 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/08/29/2025-16646/amylyx-pharmaceuticals-inc-withdrawal-of-approval-of-new-drug-application-for-relyvrio-sodium)) |
| ISRIB | Releases the brake of the integrated stress response | Preclinical (mice) | Improves memory (Sidrauski 2013); reverses age-related memory decline in old mice (Krukowski 2020) | [doi:10.7554/eLife.00498](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.00498); [doi:10.7554/eLife.62048](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.62048) |
| Restoring CMA (LAMP2A) | Chaperone-mediated autophagy | Preclinical (transgenic mice) | Better liver function in old mice | Zhang & Cuervo 2008 |
| Rapamycin, caloric restriction | mTOR → less synthesis, more autophagy | Solid preclinical; human trials (see file 05) | Indirect effects on proteostasis | Hipp 2019 |

**Finding**: real clinical successes concern **diseases targeting a single protein** (TTR, amyloid-β), with clear benefits for ATTR but modest ones for Alzheimer's. No intervention strengthens the proteostasis network as a whole in humans.

## 6. Open questions

1. **Aggregates: cause or defence?** Large aggregates could be a way of "storing away" more toxic forms (oligomers). Clearing plaques brings only a modest benefit in Alzheimer's.
2. **Global vs targeted intervention**: can HSF-1, the proteasome or CMA be boosted in humans without risk (cancers also use these networks to survive)?
3. **Biomarkers**: how to measure proteostasis in a living person, outside specific imaging (amyloid PET) or blood markers (p-tau)?
4. **Translation from the worm**: most causal evidence on longevity comes from *C. elegans* (David 2010; Walther 2015; Hsu 2003).
5. **Timing of intervention**: anti-amyloid trials suggest acting early, before symptoms.
