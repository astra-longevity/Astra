# 07 — Mitochondrial dysfunction

> "Antagonistic" hallmark (López-Otín et al., *Cell* 2023, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2022.11.001). In plain terms: mitochondria are the "power plants" of the cell. With age, they produce less energy, leak more toxic waste, and the cell is worse at recycling worn-out plants.

## 1. Definition

Mitochondria make ATP (the cell's "energy currency") through the respiratory chain. They also regulate calcium, cell death and part of cell signalling. With age, their efficiency falls, their own DNA (mtDNA) accumulates mutations, quality control (mitophagy, formation of new mitochondria) weakens and NAD+ declines. The hallmark is called "antagonistic" because at low doses, mitochondrial by-products (reactive oxygen species, ROS) are useful signals; it is their chronic excess that becomes harmful (Sun 2016, doi:10.1016/j.molcel.2016.01.028).

## 2. Mechanisms and sources

**Mitochondrial DNA mutations.** mtDNA (37 genes) is copied often and poorly protected. "Mutator" mice, whose mitochondrial DNA polymerase proofreads its copies poorly (POLG), accumulate mutations and age prematurely: hair loss, osteoporosis, anaemia, shortened lifespan (Trifunovic 2004, doi:10.1038/nature02517). This aging works mainly through cell death (apoptosis), not through excess oxidative stress (Kujoth 2005, doi:10.1126/science.1112125). In older humans, mutations are distributed as a "mosaic": some muscle fibres or neurons lose their respiratory chain and others do not (Kauppila 2017, doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.09.017).

**The free radical theory scaled back.** The old idea ("ROS damage everything, so antioxidants extend life") is largely refuted:
- low-dose ROS trigger useful defences (*mitohormesis*). Taking vitamins C and E suppresses the metabolic benefits of exercise (Ristow 2009, doi:10.1073/pnas.0903485106);
- a Cochrane meta-analysis of 78 trials concludes that high-dose beta-carotene, vitamin E and vitamin A slightly **increase** mortality (Bjelakovic 2012, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2).

**NAD+ decline.** NAD+ is essential to the respiratory chain and to sirtuins (hallmark 06). It falls with age in several tissues, partly because the enzyme CD38, carried by inflammatory immune cells, consumes more of it (Camacho-Pereira 2016, doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2016.05.006; review: Covarrubias 2021, doi:10.1038/s41580-020-00313-x).

**Failing quality control.** The cell normally removes worn-out mitochondria (mitophagy, the PINK1/Parkin pathway, whose mutations cause familial forms of Parkinson's disease: Pickrell 2015, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2014.12.007) and makes new ones (biogenesis, via PGC-1α). Both processes decline with age.

**Leaks and inflammation.** A damaged mitochondrion can release its DNA into the cell. The immune system then mistakes it for a virus and triggers an interferon response via cGAS-STING (West 2015, doi:10.1038/nature14156). This is a direct bridge to chronic inflammation.

**Sources**: sedentary lifestyle (the leading modifiable factor), overeating, drugs toxic to mitochondria, inherited mtDNA mutations.

## 3. Symptoms and clinical manifestations

- **Muscle**: the oxidative capacity of muscle falls by about 8 % per decade in humans, with less mtDNA and fewer mitochondrial proteins (Short 2005, doi:10.1073/pnas.0501559102). This contributes to fatigue, sarcopenia and exercise intolerance.
- **Brain**: Parkinson's disease (PINK1, Parkin); debated contribution to Alzheimer's.
- **Heart and vessels**: heart failure, endothelial dysfunction.
- **Metabolism**: insulin resistance, fatty liver.
- **Primary mitochondrial diseases** (rare, genetic in origin): myopathies, Barth syndrome, MELAS. They serve as "accelerated models" and led to the first approval of a drug targeting mitochondria (see §5).

## 4. Interactions with the other hallmarks


- **Cellular senescence (08)**: failing mitochondria are enough to induce senescence with a particular SASP, driven by the NAD+/NADH ratio (Wiley 2016, doi:10.1016/j.cmet.2015.11.011). Conversely, removing the mitochondria from a senescent cell erases a large part of its phenotype (Correia-Melo 2016, doi:10.15252/embj.201592862). Review: Miwa 2022, doi:10.1172/JCI158447.
- **Chronic inflammation (11)**: released mtDNA activates cGAS-STING (West 2015) and the NLRP3 inflammasome.
- **Deregulated nutrient sensing (06)**: AMPK, sirtuins and PGC-1α link available energy to mitochondrial biogenesis; shared NAD+.
- **Disabled macroautophagy (05)**: mitophagy is a form of autophagy; its decline lets worn-out mitochondria accumulate.
- **Genomic instability (01)**: mtDNA mutations are part of genomic instability; ROS also damage nuclear DNA.
- **Epigenetic alterations (03)**: intermediates of mitochondrial metabolism (acetyl-CoA, α-ketoglutarate, NAD+) feed the enzymes of the epigenome.
- **Stem cell exhaustion (09)**: in mice, restoring NAD+ revives the mitochondrial function of muscle stem cells and slightly extends life (Zhang 2016, doi:10.1126/science.aaf2693).
- **Loss of proteostasis (04)**: mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt).

## 6. Open questions

1. **Is NAD+ really limiting in humans?** Precursors do raise NAD+ in the blood, but functional gains are slim or absent. It is not yet known which tissues benefit.
2. **Cause or consequence?** In humans, mitochondrial decline largely reflects the decline in physical activity. It remains hard to separate "intrinsic" aging from a sedentary lifestyle.
3. **mtDNA mosaicism**: must a threshold of failing cells be reached for a tissue to suffer? How can it be measured in a living person?
4. **Which ROS should be reduced, where and when?** Targeted antioxidants (MitoQ, SkQ1) remain to be validated on solid clinical endpoints.
5. **Mitophagy**: does urolithin A really act through mitophagy in humans? Direct measurements in tissue are missing.
6. **Biomarkers**: there is still no standard, accessible measure of mitochondrial health (phosphorus spectroscopy, respirometry on biopsy, mtDNA in blood).

Note: a frequently cited study on PINK1/Parkin and STING inflammation (Sliter 2018, *Nature*) was **retracted**; it is not used here.
