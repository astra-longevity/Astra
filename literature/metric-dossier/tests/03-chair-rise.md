# Chair rise (sit-to-stand)

## What it measures

Leg strength and power, by standing up from a chair without using the arms. Two versions:
- **5 repeats, timed** (seconds; lower is better);
- **30 seconds, counted** (number of stands; higher is better).

## Reference population for the noise figures

- CLSA retest subsample, n ≈ 147 (Beauchamp 2021), timed version.
- Irish adults 50+ (Donoghue 2019), repeated chair stands.
- Schaun 2025 (GeroScience): three groups, middle-aged (40–55), healthy older (>60) and mobility-limited older adults, 30-s version.

## Noise

| Study | Population | Version | ICC | Smallest real change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beauchamp 2021 | CLSA | 5 repeats | 0.64 (0.45–0.77) | not taken from this source |
| Donoghue 2019 | Irish, 50+ | 5 repeats | 0.66 | 4.5 s |
| Schaun 2025 | Middle-aged | 30 s | **0.52** | reported (SEM, MDC) but not extracted here |
| Schaun 2025 | Healthy older | 30 s | 0.78 | as above |
| Schaun 2025 | Mobility-limited older | 30 s | 0.93 | as above |

- In plain English: in middle-aged healthy adults, the test is **least reliable** of all groups (ICC 0.52). For someone in the president's age band it is a weak tracker on its own.
- A **practice effect** exists: scores rise between the first and second attempt, then stabilise (Gill 2022, early knee osteoarthritis, 30-s version; that paper also reports the smallest real change for one person). Rule: discard or separately record the first attempt; always do the same number of attempts.

## Known failure modes

- Knee or hip pain.
- Chair height and protocol (arms crossed, full stand) change the score.
- Fit adults finish the 5-repeat version very fast, leaving little room to show improvement; the 30-s version suits them better.

## Functional anchor and age trajectory

Directly functional (getting up from a seat). No age-norm source checked yet for this file; the age trajectory is not stated here.

## Confounders

Body weight, leg length vs chair height, joint pain, motivation, practice.
